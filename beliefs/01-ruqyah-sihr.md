# Ruqyah / Sihr Affliction Complex — LLM Briefing

## 1. Identity Summary

The ruqyah/sihr/jinn-affliction complex is an orthodox strand of Sunni Islamic religious practice and belief centered on the reality of jinn (unseen beings made of smokeless fire), sorcery (sihr), and the evil eye ('ayn) as genuine causes of illness, misfortune, marital breakdown, and mental disturbance, paired with a prescribed cure: ruqyah, the recitation of Quranic verses and Prophetic supplications over an afflicted person or over water/oil they then consume or bathe in. Believers hold that jinn and magic are explicitly affirmed in the Quran and hadith, making disbelief in them a deviation from orthodoxy rather than a superstition layered on top of Islam. The system has an internal expert class (the raqi, or practitioner of ruqyah), a taxonomy of afflictions with named subtypes, a set of theological guardrails distinguishing permitted from forbidden remedies, and a modern institutional and media ecosystem (clinics, hijama centers, satellite TV shows, YouTube channels) that has expanded dramatically since the early 2000s. It sits in tension with folk practices (taweez amulets, zar ceremonies) that many of its own practitioners regard as corruptions bordering on shirk (associating partners with God), and with modern psychiatry over how to interpret symptoms that overlap with OCD, psychosis, and dissociative disorders.

## 2. History & Origins

Belief in jinn predates Islam; pre-Islamic Arabians regarded jinn as spirits that could inspire poets and diviners (kahin) and were both venerated and feared, with practices of seeking their aid widespread across the peninsula. Islam did not deny jinn's existence but absorbed and reordered the belief: the Quran devotes a full surah (Al-Jinn, 72) to them, affirms they are created from smokeless fire, are morally accountable, and include both believers and disbelievers, and explicitly acknowledges sihr as a real phenomenon (notably in Surah al-Baqarah 2:102, which references the story of the angels Harut and Marut in Babylon). The two final surahs of the Quran, al-Falaq and an-Nas, are understood by believers as revealed specifically as protective formulas against unseen harm, including the evil eye, envy, and the blowing of sorcerers upon knots — language drawn directly from al-Falaq's fourth verse. A foundational hadith, narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari, recounts that the Jewish sorcerer Labid ibn al-A'sam bewitched the Prophet Muhammad using a comb, strands of his hair, and the spathe of a male date palm concealed in a well, causing him temporary forgetfulness and confusion until Gabriel revealed the location and he was cured; this narrative is treated by mainstream Sunni scholarship as proof that magic can affect even a prophet's body and daily faculties without touching his prophetic message. Classical scholarship systematized the response: Ibn Taymiyyah (d. 1328) addressed jinn possession and the legal status of magic extensively in his fatwa collections, arguing that jinn could genuinely inhabit and influence humans, including through desire or infatuation, while forbidding any recourse to sorcerers. His student Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyyah devoted a section of Zad al-Ma'ad ("Provisions of the Hereafter") to Prophetic medicine, laying out the theological and therapeutic basis for treating sihr and 'ayn with Quranic recitation rather than counter-magic. The modern popular revival is usually traced to the Egyptian author Wahid Abdussalam Bali, whose book Sword Against Black Magic and Evil Magicians (published in English by Al-Firdous, London, 2004) became the de facto manual for contemporary raqis, systematizing symptom lists, named sihr categories, and step-by-step Quranic protocols. From the early 2000s onward, satellite television religious programming across the Arab world brought ruqyah practitioners into mainstream broadcast slots, and this was followed by a much larger YouTube and social-media boom in which self-taught and formally trained raqis livestream sessions, publish multi-part "jinnology" courses, and build international client bases, extending the practice well beyond its historical Middle Eastern and South Asian heartlands.

## 3. Cosmology & Causal Mechanism

Within this belief system, harm from sihr is caused by a human sorcerer (sahir) who enters into a transactional relationship with jinn, typically by performing acts of disbelief (kufr) — desecrating the Quran, invoking other than Allah, or offering sacrifices to jinn — in exchange for their service in carrying out the spell. The classic method described in the sources is the tying of knots in string or hair while breathing curses or invocations onto them (al-'uqad, referenced in Surah al-Falaq), which are then buried, hidden in the victim's home, fed to them in food or drink, or disposed of in graveyards, wells, or the sea. Physical vehicles for sihr include effigies, written talismans bearing the victim's name, and objects contaminated with the victim's bodily trace (hair, nail clippings, clothing, footprints). Separately, the evil eye ('ayn) is understood to operate without any sorcerer or deliberate spell: a person's gaze, charged with unchecked admiration, envy, or amazement at another's blessing, can transmit harm involuntarily, especially if the admirer fails to invoke a blessing formula (commonly rendered as "as God wills, there is no power except through Him") at the moment of admiration. This means 'ayn can be inflicted unintentionally, even by a loving parent or well-wisher, whereas hasad (envy) requires ill will. Jinn possession proper is explained through a doctrine of "openings": a weakened spiritual state (neglect of prayer and Quran recitation), ritual impurity, being in states of undress or using the toilet without protective invocations, strong negative emotions (anger, fear, grief), and habitual sin are all held to lower a person's spiritual defenses and create entry points jinn can exploit, sometimes at a sorcerer's direction and sometimes on a jinn's own initiative.

## 4. Entity Catalog

The believer's taxonomy of jinn draws on Quranic terms elaborated by folk and scholarly tradition. The qarin is a jinn companion assigned to every human being from birth, understood in most orthodox accounts as an inherently negative or tempting presence (paralleling the idea of a personal devil) rather than a possessing entity. Marid designates the most powerful and rebellious class of jinn, associated with defiance, while ifrit denotes a jinn of great strength and cunning, a term used in the Quran (Surah an-Naml) for a jinn who offers to fetch the Queen of Sheba's throne. Shayateen (devils) are the morally evil jinn aligned with Iblis, the jinn who refused to prostrate to Adam and became Satan; the term is used broadly for any jinn actively working to mislead or harm humans. Jinn, like humans, are held to possess free will and moral accountability, so believers distinguish Muslim jinn (who can be pious, and whom raqis may attempt to address and instruct during exorcism rather than simply expel) from kafir (disbelieving) jinn, who are considered more dangerous and resistant to Quranic recitation. A specific and heavily discussed category is jinn 'ashiq, the "lover jinn," said to become infatuated with a particular human, sometimes said by raqis to seek an ongoing relationship or even marriage with the possessed person, causing romantic aversion to human partners, nightmares of a shadowy suitor, or resistance to marriage proposals; treatment involves the raqi addressing the jinn directly during a session to instruct it that such a bond is religiously impermissible. The human sorcerer (sahir/sahirah) functions as the broker who commissions and directs jinn on behalf of a paying client, and is treated theologically as committing a major sin bordering on disbelief.

## 5. Affliction Taxonomy & Diagnostics

Practitioners in the Bali-derived tradition name several sihr subtypes by function. Sihr at-tafriq (separation magic) targets marriages and family bonds, often cited from Surah al-Baqarah 2:102, and is invoked to explain otherwise inexplicable marital conflict or divorce. Sihr al-mahabbah (love/binding magic) is its inverse, aimed at forcing obsessive attachment, frequently discussed in the context of coercive control within marriage or unwanted pursuit. Sihr at-takhyil produces false sensory impressions — making objects appear to move, distorting a person's perception of their spouse's appearance, or causing hallucination-like effects, sometimes linked to the Quranic account of Pharaoh's magicians' ropes appearing to move like snakes. Sihr al-junun is described as an attack on the nervous system producing madness-like symptoms. Sihr al-khumul causes persistent lethargy, fatigue, and loss of drive. Sihr al-hawatif produces disturbing dreams and auditory hallucinations (hearing voices). Additional categories referenced in raqi literature include sihr al-marad (illness-causing magic, producing symptoms doctors cannot diagnose) and sihr an-nazif (bleeding-related magic, associated with unexplained gynecological bleeding). 'Ayn and hasad are treated as a related but distinct category from sihr: 'ayn can strike without malice, through admiration alone, while hasad requires resentful envy; both are diagnosed by sudden onset immediately following praise or a period of visible success. Practitioners distinguish "mass" or partial affliction (sihr or 'ayn causing symptoms without full jinn residency) from full possession, in which a jinn is said to be actually inside the person's body. Waswasah refers to intrusive whispered thoughts attributed to shaytan's suggestion rather than possession, and is treated as a universal spiritual trial requiring resistance rather than exorcism. Diagnostic protocol centers on a patient's reaction while specific Quranic verses (particularly al-Falaq, an-Nas, Ayat al-Kursi, and the opening of Surah as-Saffat) are recited aloud or played: violent trembling, loss of consciousness, resistance, or a change in voice is read as confirmation of jinn presence, while recurring dreams featuring snakes, black cats, graveyards, or specific animals are treated as diagnostic signs, alongside clustered physical symptoms (chronic fatigue, unexplained pain, marital aversion) that resist conventional medical explanation.

## 6. Rules: What Is Possible vs. Impossible

The system operates under firm internal constraints. Sihr is held to be real and capable of real physical and psychological harm, but it cannot kill or permanently destroy someone Allah has decreed protection for, and nothing occurs except by Allah's ultimate permission — sihr is a means, not an independent power. Jinn cannot lawfully be commanded, bound, or employed by a Muslim; doing so is the sorcerer's sin and can itself constitute disbelief, which is why raqis insist they are not "using" jinn but rather expelling them through recitation addressed to Allah alone. Ruqyah is held to be effective only when performed with correct aqidah (creed): reciting Quran as a mechanical formula without sincere reliance on Allah, or mixing in unknown words, jinn names, astrology, or invocations to other than Allah, produces what is termed ruqyah ash-shirkiyyah (shirk-containing ruqyah), which is forbidden and considered spiritually counterproductive even if symptoms temporarily subside. Consistent practice of daily protective supplications (adhkar al-sabah wal-masaa, the morning and evening remembrances) and recitation of Ayat al-Kursi and the last verses of Surah al-Baqarah is held to confer strong, though not always described as absolute, protection against sihr and jinn intrusion. The taweez (amulet) question is the complex's sharpest internal fault line: the dominant Salafi-oriented ruqyah movement holds that wearing any amulet — even one containing Quranic text — is impermissible or outright shirk, because it implies the object itself protects rather than Allah, citing a hadith equating amulets with a broader category of forbidden practices; against this, Sufi-oriented and much folk/South Asian practice defends written Quranic taweez as legitimate when free of non-Islamic content, citing precedent among early Muslims. This is not a fringe disagreement but a live, actively argued division that shapes which raqis a given community trusts.

## 7. Expert Class & Specializations

The raqi (male) or raqiyah (female) is the recognized practitioner, and legitimacy within the community is judged by criteria such as correct Quranic recitation (tajweed), sound aqidah free of shirk-based methods, refusal to use jinn names, spirits, or unknown scripts, transparency about fees, and typically some lineage of study under recognized scholars or ruqyah centers rather than self-declared expertise. Sub-specialties have emerged within the field: some raqis focus on sihr "extraction" sessions aimed at locating and neutralizing buried talismans; others specialize in direct dialogue with the possessing jinn during exorcism, questioning it about why it entered, and in some accounts attempting to counsel a Muslim jinn toward correct belief before commanding its departure; hijama (cupping) is frequently paired with ruqyah sessions on the theory that certain sihr and 'ayn effects concentrate in specific points on the body that can be physically drawn out alongside the spiritual treatment. Regional schools differ noticeably: Saudi Arabia has moved toward institutionalizing ruqyah through licensed clerics while simultaneously running an official "Anti-Witchcraft Unit" that prosecutes practicing sorcerers, producing a state-sanctioned raqi model; Egypt retains a more informal, folk-inflected raqi culture, amplified by entertainment-adjacent TV programming; the UK and other diaspora settings have produced clinic-style ruqyah centers (often paired with hijama) operating semi-professionally with websites, bookings, and fee schedules; South Asia (Pakistan, India, Bangladesh) maintains a parallel and partly rival figure, the amil, who works within a taweez- and numerology-heavy tradition that mainstream Salafi-oriented raqis often characterize as contaminated with shirk, creating direct competition for the same clientele. Fraud is a well-documented problem: warning signs cited within the community itself include exorbitant or escalating fees (reported cases up to several thousand riyals per session), demands for exclusivity or secrecy, sealed amulets clients are told never to open, claims to name specific jinn or use jinn to fight jinn, and — in prosecuted Saudi cases — sexual abuse committed under cover of "treatment," alongside unlicensed practice.

## 8. Practices & Services (Light Side)

Preventive practice centers on the adhkar al-sabah wal-masaa (structured morning and evening remembrance texts), recitation of Ayat al-Kursi after each prayer, and the "three Quls" (al-Ikhlas, al-Falaq, an-Nas) recited and blown onto the hands then wiped over the body before sleep, all understood as forming a protective barrier against sihr, 'ayn, and jinn. Ruqyah water (and sometimes olive oil) is a core therapeutic vehicle: the raqi or the sufferer recites specified verses directly into a container of water, which is then drunk over several days and used for bathing, with sidr (lote tree) leaves sometimes crushed into the water in a practice traced to Prophetic-medicine literature and to the account of how the Prophet's own bewitchment was treated. Self-administered ruqyah (recitation without an intermediary) is generally encouraged by Salafi-oriented teaching as both effective and safer than relying on a stranger. This licit ruqyah is explicitly contrasted with ruqyah ash-shirkiyyah, incantations that invoke jinn, stars, or unknown entities, use unintelligible words, or require specific non-Quranic paraphernalia, which is considered religiously impermissible even when it produces apparent results, on the theory that any relief comes from illicit cooperation with jinn rather than divine sanction.

## 9. Demographics & Trajectory

Cross-national survey data (notably Pew Research Center's 2012 study of Muslim populations) found that in a majority of countries surveyed, half or more of Muslims affirmed that jinn and the evil eye are real, with belief highest in South and Southeast Asia — around seven in ten Muslims in South Asian countries, 84 percent in Bangladesh, and roughly three-quarters in Malaysia — and comparatively lower, though still substantial, belief in some Southeast Asian and secularized contexts. Belief in sorcery specifically was somewhat lower but still affirmed by half or more of Muslims in numerous countries surveyed. The practice has grown substantially through digital platforms since the 2000s, moving from limited local networks of trusted raqis to a global marketplace of livestreamed sessions, YouTube "jinnology" courses, and international clinic bookings, a shift researchers describe as changing both the scale and the accountability structure of the practice. This expansion runs directly into contemporary psychiatry: clinical literature on Muslim patients in transcultural psychiatric settings has found large minorities (one cited study found nearly 45 percent) attributing psychiatric symptoms to jinn, and a specific clinical debate has developed around distinguishing waswasah (religiously framed intrusive-thought struggle) from clinical OCD, with some Muslim mental health practitioners advocating integrated models in which patients pursue both ruqyah and licensed psychiatric or psychological care without treating the two as contradictory, while cautioning that normal high-frequency religious practice should not itself be pathologized as OCD.

## 10. LLM Interaction Notes

A believer within this system treats jinn, sihr, and 'ayn as established features of reality affirmed by scripture, not as "occult," "supernatural" in a fringe sense, or paranormal entertainment; framing the topic that way will read as dismissive or mocking. Core vocabulary to use accurately: ruqyah (the cure/recitation practice, not "exorcism ritual" generically), raqi/raqiyah (practitioner), sihr (sorcery, distinct from stage magic), 'ayn and hasad (evil eye and envy, kept distinct), sahir (sorcerer, always the sinning human party), taweez/tamaa'im (amulets, a contested category), and shirk (a serious theological term for associating partners with God, not used loosely). Accusing a specific named person of practicing sihr is considered a grave and potentially defamatory religious accusation within the community, comparable to a serious criminal allegation, and should not be treated lightly in generated content. Common outsider misconceptions to correct: this is not a marginal folk superstition tolerated at the edges of Islam but a position with direct Quranic and hadith textual grounding argued by mainstream classical and contemporary Sunni scholars; conversely, believers themselves aggressively police the line between orthodox ruqyah and folk practices they consider corrupted or shirk-laden, so "ruqyah" and "occult practice" are not interchangeable even within adjacent Muslim-majority folk traditions. Two adjacent-but-distinct systems are worth flagging: zar, a spirit-appeasement possession-and-healing ritual complex found around the Red Sea and Horn of Africa involving music, dance, and offerings to negotiate with possessing spirits, is explicitly and uniformly condemned by ruqyah practitioners as forbidden innovation because it treats jinn as beings to be placated rather than expelled through reliance on Allah alone; and South Asian amil/taweez culture, while overlapping in vocabulary (jinn, sihr) and clientele, is regarded by much of the Salafi-oriented ruqyah movement as a rival and partly illegitimate tradition due to its heavier reliance on written amulets, numerology, and non-Quranic formulas.

## 11. Visual Culture & Iconography

- **Practitioner appearance:** The raqi is typically a bearded man in thobe/jalabiya and taqiyah cap — or a niqab-wearing female practitioner for women patients. Sessions take place in mosques, homes, or clinics with prayer mats, a chair or foam couch, and assistants (often family) steadying the patient's shoulders or head. Female patients are treated with gloved hands or through a cloth barrier per gender-segregation norms.
- **Ritual visuals:** Loud Quranic recitation (al-Fatiha, Ayat al-Kursi, the "three Quls"); the patient may convulse, scream, gasp, or go rigid — read as the jinn reacting; the raqi presses points on the body, commands the jinn to leave ("ukhruj"), and may use olive oil, Zamzam water, or black seed.
- **Entity depictions:** Islam is broadly aniconic toward jinn — no statues or portraits exist in practice. Media render jinn as smoke or shadow figures, glowing eyes, distorted silhouettes, or purely through calligraphy; the rare figurative tradition is the Siyah Qalam demon paintings in the Topkapı albums.
- **Key artifacts:** Taweez papers with numeral grids (buduh magic squares), knotted or braided strings (the sihr of knots), buried cloth bundles containing hair or nails "discovered" during treatment, and the circular purple-red bruise pattern of hijama cupping.
