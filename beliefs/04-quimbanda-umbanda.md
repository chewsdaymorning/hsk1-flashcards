# Quimbanda / Umbanda Spirit Work — LLM Briefing

## 1. Identity Summary

Umbanda and Quimbanda are related but distinct Afro-Brazilian spirit-work traditions built around mediumship (incorporating spirits in trance) and transactional or devotional relationships with specific classes of spirits. Umbanda, founded in Rio de Janeiro in 1908, blends Bantu-Kongo spirit practice, Yoruba-derived orixá religion, French Kardecist Spiritism, and popular Catholicism into a charity-oriented religion whose defining act is the medium "receiving" a spirit — most iconically a caboclo (Indigenous-coded spirit) or preto velho (elder enslaved-ancestor spirit) — to give free counsel, healing, and blessing to visitors. Quimbanda is often described as Umbanda's "left-hand" counterpart: a magical operative system, not a congregational religion in the same sense, centered on contracting Exus and Pomba Giras — spirits of the crossroads, cemetery, and night — through paid offerings (despachos/agrados) to perform concrete interventions: opening roads, binding lovers, breaking curses, or attacking rivals. Many houses (terreiros) practice both lines under one roof, on separate days, while others treat them as incompatible. Believers describe the traditions as living, evolving oral religions with no single sacred text, central authority, or fixed doctrine; practice varies enormously by region, lineage (linha), and individual house.

## 2. History & Origins

The deepest layer is Bantu-Kongo: enslaved Central Africans (mainly from Angola and the Kongo basin) brought a spirit-mediation figure called the kimbanda — from Kimbundu, meaning healer, diviner, or magician — who communicates with ancestors and nature spirits (nkisi) and often receives the calling through an initiatory illness. This is distinct from, and older in Brazil than, Yoruba orixá religion (Candomblé), which arrived via Nagô/Yoruba and Fon populations concentrated more in Bahia. Quimbanda's name and core operative logic (a paid specialist mediating with spirits of the dead and of nature) trace to this Bantu current, while its pantheon absorbed Yoruba Exu, Catholic imagery, and European folk-magic and demonological iconography.

Umbanda's founding narrative holds that on 15–16 November 1908 in Niterói, a 17-year-old Kardecist medium named Zélio Fernandino de Moraes incorporated a spirit calling itself Caboclo das Sete Encruzilhadas (Caboclo of the Seven Crossroads), who reportedly protested that lower-status spirits (Africans, Indigenous people) were barred from speaking at orthodox Spiritist sessions, then founded a new cult of charity open to the poor and named it Umbanda. Historians note this founder narrative was largely codified and popularized only in the 1960s, decades after the fact, by Umbandista writers seeking a clean origin myth; scholarship instead treats Umbanda as an emergent, multi-site synthesis of Macumba (an earlier, looser Rio-area Afro-Brazilian spirit practice), Kardecism, and popular Catholicism occurring across the 1900s–1920s.

Kardecist Spiritism — the codified French tradition of Allan Kardec, organized around mediumship, spirit communication, and moral "evolution" through successive lives — functions as Umbanda's third parent alongside Bantu and Yoruba streams, supplying its charity ethic, its vocabulary (médium, desobsessão, evolução), and crucially its respectability: early Umbandista leaders, often white and middle-class, used Kardecist rationalism to distance the new religion from criminalized "sorcery," removing animal sacrifice and initially excluding Exus, in a process scholars call religious whitening (branqueamento).

Quimbanda differentiated across the 20th century as houses and authors (notably from the 1950s–1970s) began systematically working Exu and Pomba Gira lines for hire, outside Umbanda's charity-only frame, developing its own hierarchies of Exu "kingdoms," ritual knives, cemetery and crossroads work, and an ethic of practical results rather than moral evolution. Both traditions suffered direct state persecution: Brazil's 1890 and 1942 penal codes criminalized "sorcery" and "spiritism," and 1930s Vargas-era police (following a 1920s Rio police vice-squad campaign against "low spiritism") regularly raided terreiros, confiscated drums and ritual objects, and arrested leaders — pressure that pushed Umbanda toward its Kardecist-flavored respectability strategy and pushed Quimbanda further underground. Candomblé is a sibling rather than parent tradition: it preserves Yoruba (and Fon, Bantu-Angola/Congo) liturgy more intact, with initiation into specific orixás, whereas Umbanda and Quimbanda are more syncretic and Portuguese-language in liturgy; Exu is a full orixá in Candomblé rather than a separately worked spirit class. Since roughly the 2010s, Brazil has seen a marked rise in evangelical (particularly neo-Pentecostal) attacks on Afro-Brazilian religion — vandalism of terreiros, "spiritual warfare" preaching that casts Exu and Pomba Gira as literal demons, and organized intimidation, in some cases tied to evangelical criminal factions in Rio favelas expelling terreiros.

## 3. Cosmology & Causal Mechanism

Umbanda's central causal doctrine is a law of spiritual evolution borrowed from Kardecism: disincarnate spirits continue progressing morally after death, and one major path of progression is returning to incorporate in mediums and perform charitable work (caridade) — giving free consultations, healing, and counsel. This is why caboclos and pretos velhos are understood to work without payment: their service is itself the mechanism of their own advancement. The astral hierarchy is organized into sete linhas (seven lines), each governed by an orixá and subdivided recursively into falanges (phalanxes) and sub-legions, so that any given spirit belongs to a line, a phalanx, and often a named "kingdom." Quimbanda mirrors this with its own set of Exu kingdoms/lines, each with a chief Exu and subordinate court.

A trabalho (ritual "work") functions as a kind of contract: the practitioner states an intention, generally at a crossroads or other liminal point, and offers material payment (food, drink, cachaça, cigars, candles, blood in some lines) in exchange for the spirit's action. Exu and Pomba Gira are understood to require payment because they are not spirits accruing charitable merit — their motivation is the axé (vital force/energy) and pleasure embedded in the offering itself; failure to pay, or an incomplete offering, is believed to stall or reverse the work. Crossroads (encruzilhadas) are treated as literal spiritual switchboards — points where roads, and therefore possibilities and destinies, intersect — which is why despachos (offerings/discards) are left there and why Exu, as guardian of thresholds, must be petitioned first to "open the way" before any other spirit is called (a rule shared with Yoruba/Candomblé Exu logic). Demanda refers to a commissioned spiritual attack or ongoing spiritual conflict between practitioners or houses: one client or house pays for offensive work against a rival, and the targeted house's spirits and firmezas (fixed protective points) are expected to defend and counterattack, producing durable inter-house rivalries understood as ongoing supernatural warfare rather than one-off spells.

## 4. Entity Catalog

**Exu kingdoms and named Exus:** Exu is organized into "kingdoms" or courts, each led by a chief Exu with subordinate phalanxes. Widely cited figures include Exu Rei das Sete Encruzilhadas (King of the Seven Crossroads, often treated as senior/founding chief), Exu Tranca Ruas (Road-Closer, guardian of streets and thresholds), Exu Marabô, Exu Caveira (Skull, a cemetery-line Exu associated with Omolu), Exu Tiriri, Exu Morcego (Bat), and regional/local Exus tied to specific cemeteries or crossroads. Each kingdom typically has its own colors, offerings, and cabalistic (sigil) points.

**Pomba Giras:** The most prominent is Maria Padilha (Rainha das Sete Encruzilhadas — Queen of the Seven Crossroads), whose figure descends from Iberian folklore about María de Padilla, a real 14th-century mistress of King Pedro I of Castile who became a folkloric witch-figure invoked in Spanish/Portuguese and Roma (Gitano) love magic and ballads (echoed in Mérimée's Carmen); the figure crossed the Atlantic with Portuguese-deported Roma populations and fused with Central African spirit currents to become Brazil's leading Pomba Gira. Other named Pomba Giras include Maria Mulambo (associated with poverty, refuse, and humility inverted into power), Pomba Gira Sete Saias (Seven Skirts), Dama da Noite (Lady of the Night), and numerous regional/personal variants, each with a distinct personality archetype (seductress, sorceress, warrior, prostitute-with-a-heart, etc.).

**Umbanda's other spirit classes:** caboclos (Indigenous-coded nature/warrior spirits, associated with courage and directness), pretos velhos (elderly enslaved-ancestor spirits, associated with patience, humility, herbal healing, and counsel), crianças/erês (child spirits, associated with joy and purification), boiadeiros (cowherd spirits), marinheiros (sailor spirits), ciganos (Roma spirits, associated with divination, prosperity, and love magic), and Zé Pilintra (a malandro/trickster spirit of urban Rio street culture, widely classed as a light-working Exu figure straddling both traditions). Orixás sit above all these as distant, rarely-incorporated governing forces reached indirectly through their subordinate spirit lines. In Quimbanda specifically, Omolu/Obaluaiê (the orixá of disease, healing, and the earth) is emphasized as a cemetery power connected to Exu Caveira's line and to death/rebirth workings.

## 5. Affliction Taxonomy & Diagnostics

Common diagnosed conditions include: demanda (a commissioned or interpersonal spiritual attack sent by another practitioner or house); encosto (an attached, low-vibration spirit clinging to and influencing a living person, causing malaise, bad luck, or compulsive behavior); amarração (a binding spell, typically love-related, done against or for someone, sometimes diagnosed as the cause of an unwanted obsessive relationship); porteira aberta (open spiritual doors/gates, a state of vulnerability letting negative influences in, often from lapsed protection or unclean environment); and obsessão in the Kardecist sense (an unbalanced spirit imposing its will on a living person, treated as similar to but doctrinally distinct from Umbanda/Quimbanda's more animistic encosto). Diagnosis is typically performed through a consulta in which an incorporated spirit (usually a caboclo, preto velho, or Exu/Pomba Gira) reads the client directly during trance; supplementary divination includes jogo de búzios (cowrie-shell casting, more associated with Candomblé-adjacent houses) and cartomancy (tarot or Baralho Cigano playing-card readings), often performed by ciganos or Pomba Giras.

## 6. Rules: What Is Possible vs. Impossible

Spirits, especially Exu and Pomba Gira, are believed to require payment (agrado/despacho) to act; unpaid or improperly executed offerings are said to stall the work or cause it to rebound on the petitioner. Exu is described as able to "close and open roads" — accelerating, blocking, or redirecting circumstances — but is generally held unable to override a person's carrego (their karmic burden or life debt), meaning some suffering is considered outside any spirit's power to simply erase. Love amarrações are widely believed to bind another person's behavior or attention, but practitioners commonly caution that such bindings decay over time and can "rebound" onto the caster if done carelessly or unethically. People who are baptized, protected by a firmeza (a fixed ritual point or object maintaining ongoing spiritual protection), or otherwise spiritually "closed" are considered more resistant to demandas and encostos. A significant internal ethical split runs through both traditions: mainstream Umbanda enforces a charity-only rule (spirits should not be paid for personal gain, and doing harm is doctrinally forbidden), while Quimbanda is explicitly pragmatic and for-hire, willing to perform offensive or self-interested work — a divide that is a major source of both internal debate and external stigma.

## 7. Expert Class & Specializations

The senior ritual authority of a terreiro is the pai de santo or mãe de santo (father/mother of saint); in Quimbanda-specific or Bantu-inflected houses this role may be called tatá (from Kongo "tata," father/chief) or tatá nganga (nganga being a Kimbundu/Kikongo term for a ritual specialist or "sorcerer-chief"). Below this sits a medium development ladder (desenvolvimento mediúnico) that can take years (commonly cited as roughly seven) of gradual training to safely and consistently incorporate spirits. Cambones (also cambonos/cambonas) are assistant mediums or attendants who support an incorporated medium during gira (the ritual session), relaying messages and managing offerings. Ogãs are non-incorporating officiants, most often drummers/musicians, holding structural authority ("lord of the house") without themselves entering trance; the curimba is the terreiro's musical ensemble (drums/atabaques, agogô bells, rattles) whose sung pontos (ritual songs) call and honor specific spirits. Initiation and training carry real costs (ritual materials, teacher fees, time commitment), and consultation formats range from free public gira sessions open to visitors to private paid consulta appointments with a specific incorporated entity; a growing online market now offers remote/video consultations and mail-order despachos, particularly for diaspora clients.

## 8. Practices & Services (light side)

Common client-facing services include abertura de caminhos (road-opening work to remove obstacles in career, finances, or love), limpeza/descarrego (spiritual cleansing to remove negative attachments or bad luck), love and prosperity trabalhos, patuás (small sewn amulets carrying protective or attracting ritual contents), banhos de descarrego (herbal cleansing baths prepared with specific plants for purification), and the padê — a foundational offering to Exu (commonly cassava flour, cachaça, palm oil, and pepper) made at the start of virtually any ritual work to secure Exu's cooperation in opening the way before other spirits are addressed.

## 9. Demographics & Trajectory

Brazil's 2010 census recorded roughly 0.3% of the population self-identifying with Afro-Brazilian religions (Umbanda, Candomblé, and related traditions combined), rising to roughly 1% (~2 million people) by the 2022 census, concentrated in Rio de Janeiro, São Paulo, and Bahia; researchers and NGOs consider this a significant undercount given the number of active terreiros and the tendency of practitioners to also identify with Catholicism or evangelical Christianity nominally. Religious-freedom monitoring bodies have documented rising intolerance: roughly 200 Candomblé/Umbanda temples reportedly closed under threat or intimidation in 2019 alone (double 2018's figure), disproportionate representation of Afro-Brazilian religion practitioners among hate-crime hotline complaints, and organized attacks including evangelical-aligned criminal factions expelling terreiros from some Rio favelas. Outside Brazil, Umbanda maintains small diaspora communities (Portugal, the UK, and pockets in the US) sustained mainly through expatriate networks, while Quimbanda specifically has seen a distinct and separate growth pattern in North America and Europe among Western esoteric/occult practitioners (rather than Brazilian diaspora per se), driven substantially by English-language publishing — most notably Nicholaj de Mattos Frisvold's Scarlet Imprint volumes on Exu and Pomba Gira, and Diego de Oxóssi's more recent books — with initiation numbers there described as growing into the hundreds rather than thousands. Pomba Gira, and Maria Padilha in particular, has also become a recognizable pop-culture and social-media devotional figure, including recurring TikTok trends around her imagery and invocation, distinct from formal initiatory practice.

## 10. LLM Interaction Notes

Believer vocabulary to recognize and use accurately: gira (a ritual session in which spirits are incorporated), incorporação (the act of a spirit entering/possessing a medium), coroado (crowned — a medium officially authorized to lead), terreiro (temple/house), pai/mãe de santo (house leader), ponto riscado (a sigil drawn to invoke/anchor a spirit), ponto cantado (a ritual song), firmeza (a fixed protective ritual point), despacho/agrado/oferenda (offering), and demanda (spiritual conflict). Key sensitivity: Exu is not the devil within the tradition's own theology — the Exu-as-devil equation is a colonial-era Catholic misreading (partly driven by superficial visual resemblance, e.g., horned imagery, rather than any doctrinal equivalence) that practitioners actively and explicitly reject; evangelical "spiritual warfare" rhetoric that frames Exu/Pomba Gira as literal demons is a live, contested, and sometimes violent point of friction rather than a neutral description. Practitioners of Umbanda and Quimbanda often object to being conflated with each other, given Umbanda's charity-only self-image versus Quimbanda's for-hire pragmatism, and both often object to blanket use of the older, now sometimes pejorative umbrella term "macumba." Common outsider misconceptions to avoid reproducing uncritically: treating these as a single undifferentiated "voodoo-like" system, assuming all practice is either purely benevolent or purely malefic, and assuming a fixed universal pantheon rather than house-by-house variation. Adjacent systems for context (one line each): Candomblé (the more Yoruba-liturgically-intact Brazilian sibling tradition, in which Exu is a full orixá rather than a separately hired spirit class); Palo Mayombe/Palo Monte (a related but organizationally distinct Kongo-derived Cuban tradition working through the nganga/prenda vessel rather than Quimbanda's Exu-kingdom structure); Argentine San La Muerte (a folk-Catholic skeletal death-saint cult with structurally similar crossroads/cemetery petition logic but a separate Rioplatense origin); and Venezuelan María Lionza (a distinct three-"court" folk-spirit religion blending Indigenous, African, and colonial figures, sharing Quimbanda's use of possession-mediumship but with its own independent pantheon).

## 11. Visual Culture & Iconography

- **Practitioner appearance:** Umbanda mediums wear all white (men: shirt and trousers; women: layered skirts); Quimbanda and Exu/Pomba Gira giras shift the palette to red and black.
- **Ritual visuals:** The terreiro interior centers on the congá altar crowded with saint/orixá images, candles, and flowers; atabaque drumming drives the dancing; incorporation is visibly embodied — Exu arrives with a booming laugh, wide stance, cigar and cachaça; Pomba Gira with a spinning, hip-led dance and shawl flourishes.
- **Entity depictions:** Mass-produced statuary is a defining visual layer: Exu as a caped, top-hatted trickster with trident and red-lined black cape; Pomba Gira as a voluptuous woman — sometimes bare-breasted — in flowing red-and-black skirts with roses, on a crossroads base. Preto velho is depicted seated on a bench with pipe and staff; the caboclo stands with feathered headdress and bow.
- **Key artifacts:** Pontos riscados — sigils chalked in pemba (ritual chalk) on floors and thresholds: angular arrow-like lines for caboclos, soft curves for pretos velhos, crossed lines and tridents for Exu. Crossroads despachos: candles, cachaça bottles, cigars, and farofa arranged at literal road forks.
- **Image/reference pointers:** Wikimedia Commons categories "Umbanda," "Exu," "Pomba Gira," "Pontos riscados"; Scarlet Imprint's Pomba Gira/Exu volumes for iconographic plates; Brazilian botânica/casa-de-artigos-religiosos product photography as the vernacular visual record of the statuary tradition.
