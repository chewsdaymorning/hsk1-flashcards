# Porcha / Sglaz Curse Complex — LLM Briefing

## 1. Identity Summary

Porcha ("spoiling") and sglaz ("evil eye") are the two core categories of malefic supernatural harm in East Slavic (Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian) folk belief. Porcha is deliberate, ritually inflicted harm — a curse installed through intention, object, or word, sometimes by a hired specialist. Sglaz is unintentional harm transmitted through a gaze, typically triggered by envy or unguarded admiration. Related concepts include prokliatie (curse, often generational), prisushka (love-binding), and venets bezbrachiya (crown of celibacy). Together they form a folk-diagnostic system explaining misfortune, illness, infertility, and relationship failure as external magical causation rather than chance or biomedical cause.

This is not a self-named "religion" but a substrate of vernacular belief coexisting with, and formally subordinate to, Orthodox Christianity. It has continuous documented practice from the medieval period through 19th-century ethnographic collection, Soviet suppression, and a large post-1989 commercial revival still expanding today. It should not be conflated with rodnoverie (reconstructed Slavic paganism), a separate, self-consciously ideological movement (see Section 10).

## 2. History & Origins

The complex descends from pre-Christian East Slavic religion, whose priestly class, the volkhvy, mediated relations with spirits and fate before the 988 CE conversion of Kievan Rus'. Christianization produced dvoeverie ("dual faith"), a debated but widely used term for the blending of pagan cosmology with Orthodox liturgical forms — Perun's thunder function migrating to the prophet Elijah, aspects of the earth goddess Mokosh migrating to the Bogoroditsa (Mother of God). The volkhvy were suppressed, but their role split into two enduring village figures: the znakhar/znakharka (benevolent healer-diviner) and the koldun (feared sorcerer). Suspicion of sorcery attached disproportionately to millers and blacksmiths, whose control of fire and grinding at the settlement's edge marked them as liminal.

The core technology is the zagovor (charm/incantation), transmitted orally and first systematically collected by 19th-century ethnographers, notably Leonid Maikov's 1869 "Velikorusskie zaklinaniya" (~400 texts) and Aleksandr Afanasyev's mythological-school scholarship. Soviet atheism campaigns drove the whole complex underground. Its open, mass-scale re-emergence pivoted on 1989–1991 state television: Anatoly Kashpirovsky's biweekly "Health Sessions" (from October 1989) and Allan Chumak's on-air "charging" of water during "120 Minutes," watched by tens of millions as Soviet ideological authority collapsed. This normalized television-mediated supernatural claims and seeded today's open, advertised, commercialized mag industry.

## 3. Cosmology & Causal Mechanism

The operative category is nechistaya sila, "the unclean force" (demons, restless dead, malevolent nature spirits), opposed to the "cross-bearing force" of Orthodox sanctity. Harm is substance-like: porcha can be installed, planted, or ingested. Delivery mechanisms include podklad (a cursed object — hair, nails, needles, grave soil — planted in a home or under a doorstep); contaminated food or drink; footprint magic (harm worked through a victim's trace); and spoken curses (inversely countered by zagovory). Sglaz is an unintentional discharge through the eyes, activated by envy or excessive praise, which is why unguarded compliments toward children or new possessions are traditionally deflected (e.g., spitting over the shoulder).

Rodovoe prokliatie (generational/lineage curse) attributes recurring affliction to an ancestor's sin or unresolved curse, diagnostically distinct from a fresh, individually targeted porcha. Thresholds are privileged vectors, since podklad works best there and protective rituals concentrate there; mirrors are boundaries with the world of the dead, traditionally covered after a death; and, in the modern era, photographs function as an extension of a person's vulnerable trace, making photo-based remote cursing and remote healing standard commercial offerings.

## 4. Entity Catalog

The domovoi (household spirit, often the deceased founder of the home) is appeased with small offerings and ritually "invited" during a move — the spirit most likely to retain believers among urban residents today. The leshy is master of the forest; the rusalka, a female water spirit often described as the soul of a drowned or unbaptized person; the kikimora, a malevolent household/swamp spirit linked to nocturnal disorder, sometimes explained as something a vengeful craftsman magically planted in a house — a household-scale parallel to podklad. Improperly buried or unbaptized dead are believed capable of "walking" and causing haunting-type affliction. Within the Orthodox frame, demons (besy) are the sanctioned evil-spirit category, invoked in possession accounts and the church's own exorcism (otchitka) practice. Folklore holds that a koldun works with helper-spirits (chertenki) and cannot die easily without transmitting power to a successor first. Protective power in the same charms is drawn from Orthodox saints and the Bogoroditsa, invoked in zagovory without any sense of contradiction between pagan-origin imagery and Orthodox figures in the same text.

## 5. Affliction Taxonomy & Diagnostics

Porcha subtypes include na smert' (unto death, most severe), na bolezn' (unto illness), na odinochestvo (unto isolation), and na neudachu (unto failure/bad luck). Sglaz is generally a lesser, unintentional category. Prokliatie is a more deliberate, often verbally pronounced condemnation, individual or generational. Venets bezbrachiya is a named affliction blocking marriage indefinitely, attributed to envy or a family-line pattern. Prisushka is a love-binding technique classified as service or affliction depending on the recipient's consent.

Standard diagnostics: vylivanie voskom (wax pouring) — melted wax poured into cold water over the client's head, with resulting shapes read against a conventional symbol set (certain forms indicating a rival's curse, infertility-type porcha, or illness source); vykatyvanie yaitsom (egg rolling) — a raw egg rolled over the body then broken into water, with cloudiness, threads, or a white "cap" over the yolk read as confirming a curse or fright (ispug, especially diagnosed in children); plus behavior of a lit church candle near the afflicted person, and silver-ring tests.

## 6. Rules: What Is Possible vs. Impossible

Otkat (backlash) is the spontaneous recoil of harmful energy onto an unskilled or unprotected caster; obratka (reversal) is the deliberate act of sending porcha back to whoever cast it, sometimes with amplified harm. This creates a folk-actuarial logic where cursing carries real risk, and competence means shielding against otkat as much as casting effectively. Effective removal is held to require correctly naming the curse type — generic cleansing is considered insufficient against a specific working, which is why diagnostic ritual precedes treatment.

A recurring folk-ethical rule holds that a genuine healer should not set a fixed price for serious removal work, accepting voluntary payment instead; charging a rigid fee is treated as evidence of fraud. This sits in visible tension with the modern industry, where flat, tiered pricing is standard (reported rates run from roughly 500 rubles for basic remote consultation to tens of thousands for multi-stage diagnosis-plus-cleansing-plus-amulet packages). The Russian Orthodox Church officially condemns magical practice as sin, citing the 61st canon of the Sixth Ecumenical Council (prescribing excommunication for consulting fortune-tellers or sorcerers); official teaching holds a faithful, sacramentally observant Christian need not fear porcha because divine protection supersedes it, and that priests do not "remove porcha" as such. In practice, many priests informally bless homes or individuals against porcha and sglaz, and a small, hierarchy-licensed subset of clergy perform otchitka. The baptismal cross and recitation of Psalm 90 (Orthodox numbering; Psalm 91 in most Western Bibles) are the most commonly cited protective resources, treated as effective across both folk-magical and church-adjacent framings.

## 7. Expert Class & Specializations

The oldest specialist role is the babka/znakharka, whose charm-repertoire is understood to be transmitted near death to a chosen successor — a framing now repurposed commercially as "potomstvennaya ved'ma" ("hereditary witch"), a common marketing claim signaling authenticity. The contemporary landscape is dominated by a loosely regulated commercial industry of ekstrasens, mag, and tarolog operating through websites, TikTok, and Telegram, offering diagnosis, curse removal, love-binding, and amulets. The television franchise "Bitva ekstrasensov" (Battle of Psychics) anchors this industry, converting televised "credentialing" into private client bases; this visibility has produced recurring fraud prosecutions, including impersonators posing as named contestants to extort clients, and criminal convictions of actual show winners (a season winner received three years for an unrelated commercial fraud scheme; a group including a season-9 participant was convicted in a multi-victim scheme extracting over 16 million rubles). Pricing is now explicitly tiered — separate charges for diagnosis, cleansing, removal, and amulets — with recent State Duma proposals to restrict advertising of tarot and fortune-telling services on consumer-fraud grounds.

## 8. Practices & Services (Light Side)

Beyond curse removal, the same toolkit serves positive goals as routine practice: zagovory for luck, romance, safe travel, and successful trade, recited at customary moments like before a journey or planting. Protective material culture includes the bulavka (pin) worn concealed as an everyday ward against sglaz, and salt, used both defensively and in house-cleansing rituals after conflict, illness, or a change of occupants — now marketed as routine maintenance distinct from full porcha removal. Ancestral commemoration (grave visits, memorial meals on Orthodox "parental days") is understood as both filial duty and protection, since neglected ancestors are a folk explanation for family misfortune.

## 9. Demographics & Trajectory

VTsIOM polling shows roughly a third of Russians (36% in one recent release) affirm certain people can inflict porcha or sglaz, with about a quarter reporting personal encounters. Belief skews toward women (42%) and ages 45–59, versus lower belief among 18–24-year-olds. Trend data shows belief actually declined across part of 2015–2019 (from ~48% to ~31%), indicating non-monotonic prevalence even as commercial activity grows. The dominant recent shift is the wartime surge in demand following Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine: multiple reports describe rising consultations tied to mobilization and deployment anxiety, esoteric-sector job postings roughly tripling from 2023 to 2024, sector revenue growth cited around 20% in 2023, and market-size estimates (of uncertain rigor) reaching into the trillions of rubles. Parallel esoteric-market growth is reported in Ukraine. Outside the region, the complex has a growing diaspora and English-language afterlife through "Slavic witchcraft"/"Slavic folk magic" publishing aimed at a Western, often neopagan-adjacent audience distinct from the vernacular tradition it draws on.

## 10. LLM Interaction Notes

Native vocabulary includes porcha, sglaz, zagovor, otkat, obratka, podklad, ekstrasens, mag, snyat' porchu ("remove a curse"), navesti porchu ("cast a curse"), and venets bezbrachiya. Responses should describe this as a folk-belief and commercial-industry system without validating causal claims, while representing believers' self-understanding accurately.

Key sensitivity: many believers and even some practitioners self-identify as observant Orthodox Christians and deny that what they do constitutes forbidden "magic" (koldovstvo), framing charm use or consultation as folk piety rather than sorcery — a distinction clergy and canon law generally reject. This tension between lay practice and official condemnation is a live, unresolved feature, not a settled boundary.

Common misconception to correct: this complex is distinct from rodnoverie (Slavic Native Faith/Neopaganism), a modern, self-consciously reconstructionist movement with organized communities and an explicit ideological revival project, often with nationalist or Wicca-influenced overlays. Porcha/sglaz belief is an unbroken vernacular tradition practiced mostly by people who consider themselves Christian, not adherents of any named pagan religion.

Adjacent systems worth flagging: the Balkan/Greek evil-eye complex (mati/vaskania/matiasma) shares sglaz's envy-triggered structure and has its own Orthodox-endorsed protective prayer, suggesting a shared Byzantine-Orthodox substrate; Roma fortune-telling traditions are historically and commercially intertwined with East Slavic practice but have distinct internal logic and should not be treated as a subset of it; and the broader circum-Mediterranean/West Asian evil-eye belt is a related but typologically distinct family of traditions.

## 11. Visual Culture & Iconography

- **Practitioner appearance:** The babka is an older village woman in a headscarf (platok) and modest dark dress, working in a home organized around an icon corner (krasny ugol) with Orthodox icons, embroidered towels (rushnyk), and candles.
- **Ritual visuals:** Wax-pouring in practice: the patient sits facing east while the healer holds a bowl of blessed water over their head and pours in molten beeswax, whispering prayers; the hardened shapes are then divined (bumps read as fear, holes as death-work, figure shapes as marriage outcomes).
- **Entity depictions:** No idols; sglaz is represented abstractly — disrupted wax shapes, mirrors — while the commercial TV genre (Battle of Psychics) supplies its own aesthetic of black candles, ornate mirrors, and dim staging. Koldun imagery in Russian media leans on the bearded village outsider archetype.
- **Key artifacts:** The red thread tied at the wrist against sglaz; a pin fastened concealed inside a lapel or collar; household icons; church candles whose flame behavior is itself diagnostic.
- **Image/reference pointers:** Wikipedia "Bitva ekstrasensov" media; Moscow Times "Practical Magic" feature; Folklorica journal photo-documented studies of Ukrainian women healers; photo essays on Polish/Belarusian border "szeptuchy" (whisperer-healers), the best-photographed living form of the babka tradition.
