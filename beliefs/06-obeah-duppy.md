# Obeah / Duppy Work — LLM Briefing

## 1. Identity Summary

Obeah is a Caribbean tradition of ritual practice — protective, healing, and harmful — descended from West African spiritual technologies and reshaped under slavery, primarily in the Anglophone Caribbean (Jamaica above all, but also Trinidad, Guyana, Barbados, the Bahamas, and the Eastern Caribbean). It is not a religion with a fixed pantheon, congregation, or clergy; it is closer to a body of applied ritual technique — divination, charm-making, herbal medicine, and spirit work — practiced by individual specialists on a client basis. Duppy belief is the closely linked folk cosmology of the dead: the conviction that a residual spirit-shadow of a deceased person (the duppy) can linger, be provoked, or be deliberately manipulated by a ritual specialist to help or harm the living. Obeah and duppy lore function together as a single explanatory and practical system for illness, misfortune, justice, and protection, historically criminalized by British colonial law and still legally and socially contested today.

## 2. History & Origins

Scholars trace obeah's roots to West African healing-and-harming complexes carried by enslaved Africans, with two competing (not mutually exclusive) etymological theories. The Akan theory derives "obeah" from Twi terms such as *obayifo* (a person, often malevolent, believed to possess supernatural power) or *bayi* (witchcraft), reflecting the large Gold Coast (Akan/Ashanti) presence among enslaved Jamaicans. The Igbo theory derives it from *dibia* (an Igbo healer, diviner, or prophet) or the related *obia/abia* found among Igbo, Ibibio, and Efik speakers of the Bight of Biafra, terms with positive connotations of healing and socially sanctioned spiritual power. No single etymology commands scholarly consensus; the ambiguity itself mirrors obeah's dual reputation as both healing craft and feared sorcery.

Obeah entered the historical record forcefully through **Tacky's Rebellion** (1760), Jamaica's largest 18th-century slave uprising, in which obeah practitioners administered oaths and protective rituals drawing on Akan spiritual knowledge to bind fighters and promise invulnerability. In direct response, Jamaica's colonial assembly passed the first anti-obeah law later in 1760 as part of a broader slave-control act, making the practice punishable by death or transportation. This made obeah the target of sustained legal suppression precisely because colonial authorities recognized it as an engine of resistance and solidarity. Subsequent amendments (1857, 1892, 1893) tightened enforcement, culminating in the **Obeah Act of 1898**, designed to make convictions easier to secure and to sweep in a broad range of folk-healing and divinatory activity, not just malicious sorcery.

Historically, obeah has been paired with its dyadic counterpart, **Myal**, an African-Jamaican ritual-dance and spirit-mediumship complex oriented toward healing, protection, and the detection and neutralization of obeah's harmful workings. Where obeah could be commissioned to curse or harm, Myal mediums positioned themselves as "spiritual police," rooting out obeah charms and countering curses through trance, dance, and invocation of ancestral power. This obeah/Myal opposition — sorcery versus its ritual antidote — structured 18th- and 19th-century Afro-Jamaican religious life. In the 19th century, Myalism fed directly into the Christian revivals of 1860–61, producing **Revival Zion** (and its more Africanized sibling, Pocomania/Pukumina), in which former Myal practitioners folded Myal's healing and anti-witchcraft mission into a Bible-centered, spirit-possession Christianity that still positions itself as obeah's rival and moral opposite.

Two related but historically distinct Kongo-derived traditions sit adjacent to this obeah/Myal/Revival complex: **Kumina**, brought to eastern Jamaica by indentured Central African "Congos" after emancipation, centered on ancestor communication through drumming, chanting, and possession; and **Convince** (also called Bongo or Flenkey), a Kongo-influenced tradition strongest among Jamaican Maroon communities, similarly built around ancestral spirit possession. Both are now recognized as part of Jamaica's official intangible cultural heritage.

Between 1890 and 1939, newspaper-based research documents roughly 813 obeah-related arrests/prosecutions in Jamaica and 121 in Trinidad, with conviction rates around 81% and 78% respectively — evidence of sustained, effective 20th-century legal persecution carried out mostly in low-level magistrates' courts whose records survive chiefly through press coverage. A decriminalization wave followed regional independence: Anguilla (1980), Barbados (1998), Trinidad and Tobago (2000), and St. Lucia (2004) all repealed their anti-obeah statutes. Jamaica has not. In 2025, University of the West Indies professor Clinton Hutton, represented by attorney Bert Samuels, filed a constitutional challenge in Jamaica's Supreme Court arguing the 1898 Obeah Act violates rights to freedom of religion, conscience, expression, and privacy; the case was catalyzed by the prosecution of a man named Oshawn Grant over ritual items found in his home. The first hearing occurred in September 2025, accompanied by both advocacy vigils from Africana-spiritualist groups (notably the Unuchi Foundation) and organized protest marches by Christian church leaders opposed to decriminalization.

## 3. Cosmology & Causal Mechanism

Duppy belief rests on a folk concept of multiple souls, likely derived from Akan and/or Bantu (Kongo) precedents. In the classic formulation recorded from early-20th-century informants, a person has two spirits: one "from God," good, which ascends to heaven (often said to rise on the third day, sometimes via a tree); and another spirit "not from God," which may remain on earth as the duppy — an amoral, potentially dangerous residue of the personality that persists after death if not properly dismissed. The **nine-night wake** (Nine Night, Dead Yard, Set-Up) exists precisely to perform this dismissal: nine nights of communal gathering, song, storytelling, food, and ritual are meant to honor the dead and send the duppy off so it does not linger to trouble the living; a duppy sent off correctly at Nine Night is believed not to return.

A duppy can be deliberately "caught" and "set on" a target by a ritual specialist. Reported methods include mixing graveyard dirt with unsalted rice and rum and depositing it at the root of a cotton (silk-cotton/kapok) tree — the tree where duppies are believed to congregate — and leaving silver coins at a grave as payment or binding to enlist a spirit's cooperation. This "setting" of a duppy on a person is sometimes called delivering a "blow" or a "set hand." Sympathetic transfer is a related mechanism: personal items (hair, clothing, nail clippings, footprints) are used to link a working to a specific victim, on the logic that harm done to the item or a representation of the person transfers to them. "Guzum" (also guzzu) is a Jamaican folk term for this broader occult-working complex, sometimes used interchangeably with obeah, science, or vodou/voodoo in popular speech. Underlying all of this is a shadow-catching logic: the duppy is understood as an earthly shadow-self capable of being captured, bound, directed, and weaponized by someone with the requisite knowledge — a technology of the dead rather than a matter of worship.

## 4. Entity Catalog

Jamaican duppy typology includes named figures with specific behaviors and countermeasures. The **Rolling Calf** is a chain-dragging, fire-eyed calf-like beast said to be the duppy of a wicked person in life (butchers especially), which blocks roads at night; travelers escape by dropping objects for it to count, reaching a crossroads first, or sticking an open knife into the ground. The **Three-Foot Horse** gallops faster than any living horse and kills with poisonous breath, but cannot attack anyone standing in a tree's shadow. The **Whooping Boy**, said to be the tortured soul of an enslaved person, rides the Three-Foot Horse and haunts wooded areas. **River Mumma** is a female water-spirit who guards river sources, appearing as a woman combing her hair with a golden comb left as bait to draw victims into the water.

In Trinidad and Guyana, the **soucouyant** (Guyanese: Ol' Higue) is a shape-shifting vampiric hag who sheds her skin at night, stores it in a mortar, and flies as a fireball to suck blood from sleeping victims, entering homes through tiny cracks; she is countered by finding and salting her discarded skin, or by scattering rice/sand at doorways she must stop to count. **Jumbie** is the generic Guyanese/Trinidadian term for malevolent spirits broadly, encompassing several subtypes: **douens** (dwens), the faceless spirits of unbaptized dead children, recognizable by backward-facing feet and broad hats hiding their featureless faces; **la diablesse**, a devil-woman who lures men at night, concealing a cloven hoof beneath a long white dress; and the **moko jumbie**, a stilt-walking figure whose towering height originated in West African ritual practice as a guardian who wards off evil spirits during festivals, now a staple of Caribbean carnival performance. The **bacoo** (baccoo) of Guyana is a bottle-imp — a small spirit kept corked in a rum bottle who grants wishes and performs mischief or labor in exchange for food, but who is said to eventually turn on and torment its keeper. Alongside these folkloric entities, Revival Zion and related traditions invoke biblical spirits — archangels, prophets, apostles — as protective forces summoned in trance to counter obeah workings and duppy attacks.

## 5. Affliction Taxonomy & Diagnostics

Folk diagnosis distinguishes ordinary illness from spiritually caused affliction. "Duppy sickness" encompasses wasting illness, sudden madness, and being "struck" or put in the "duppy box" — a state of spiritual affliction or torment attributed to a duppy or demon set upon a person. A "set hand" or "blow" refers to a spell or curse delivered by a ritual specialist on a client's behalf. Related folk-diagnostic categories include being "salted" or "crossed," terms for a condition of accumulated bad luck or spiritual contamination requiring ritual cleansing. Diagnosis is typically performed by an obeah practitioner or a Revival "reader"/Mother/Shepherd through several recognized methods: direct spiritual "reading" of the client (often via trance or intuited insight), interpretation of dream messages, key-and-Bible divination (a key inserted into a Bible tied with thread, the book's turning motion read as answer while a psalm, commonly Psalm 50, is recited), and observation of a glass of water (used to "read" for spiritual disturbance or to locate a problem).

## 6. Rules: What Is Possible vs. Impossible

Folk protective logic follows consistent rules. Duppies are widely held to be unable to cross running water, and to be compelled to stop and count anything scattered before them — rice, sand, gravel — which is why scattering these substances at doorways or behind a fleeing person is a standard defense; some accounts hold duppies cannot count past a certain small number, forcing them to restart endlessly. Silver coins and salt function as protective counters and payments in duppy transactions alike. Red paint, tar, or a cross marked on doorframes are used to bar duppy entry into homes. Those born with a caul (membrane) over the face, and so-called "four-eye" people, are traditionally believed immune to duppy harm and able to see duppies invisible to others — a trait also associated with the "gift" that marks out a potential obeah practitioner, historically termed doctor, professor, one-eye man, or doctorman. A properly conducted Nine Night is believed to permanently prevent a duppy's return, which is why the ritual's correct performance is taken so seriously. Twins are also traditionally held to be immune to duppy mischief. Baptism (particularly Revival-style water baptism) is held to confer lasting protection against spiritual attack.

Obeah itself is understood as morally neutral — a technology rather than a doctrine — so the same practitioner may be hired to protect a client one day and attack a rival the next; this neutrality is part of what distinguishes obeah from the explicitly anti-witchcraft mission of Myal and Revival healers. Protective charms and "guard rings" placed around a property or person are not permanent; they must be periodically "fed" (with rum, food offerings, or renewed ritual) to remain effective. A recognized limit on obeah's power is imported European ceremonial/grimoire magic, popularly called "**higher science**" or simply "**de Laurence**" — a reference to the Chicago-based L. W. de Laurence publishing and occult mail-order house, whose books on divination, hoodoo, and magic were so pervasive in the early-20th-century Caribbean that the name became shorthand for a superior, more dangerous order of occult power. British colonial authorities banned import, sale, and possession of de Laurence publications as "instruments of Obeah"; independent Jamaica lifted many such bans in the 1970s but pointedly kept the de Laurence ban in place, and it remains illegal to import these materials into Jamaica today. Practitioners associated with de Laurence-derived "science" are viewed with particular suspicion and operate with more secrecy than ordinary obeah workers.

## 7. Expert Class & Specializations

The core practitioner is the obeahman or obeahwoman, historically also called doctorman, do-good man, professor, or scientist — the last term specifically denoting a grimoire-literate practitioner working within the de Laurence/"higher science" lineage rather than purely African-derived technique. Operating in parallel and partial rivalry are Revival Zion figures — the Mother (Madda), Shepherd, Captain, or Balmist — who present themselves as Christian healers combating obeah and duppy affliction through prayer, hymn, and trance rather than sorcery, even though outsiders sometimes conflate their practice with obeah itself. Practitioners often work from a **balmyard**, a home compound identifiable by colored ritual flags, where bush baths, herbal treatments, chants, and consultations are conducted. In Trinidad and Guyana, the practitioner landscape is denser still, overlapping with Orisha (Shango) priests, Spiritual Baptist "mothers" and "leaders," and Guyanese Comfa/Komfa workers who mediate between clients and an inventory of ancestral, ethnic, and deity spirits. Recruitment into practice is understood less as formal training than as a spiritual calling or inherited gift — being born with a caul, being a "four-eye" child who sees duppies, or receiving the gift through family lineage or dream-instruction. Payment is typically a direct client fee for a specific service (a reading, a working, a bath), transactional rather than tithe-based, reflecting obeah's character as commissioned ritual labor rather than congregational religion.

## 8. Practices & Services (light side)

Common client-facing services include the construction and maintenance of protective "guard rings" around homes, businesses, or persons; bush baths using specific herb combinations for cleansing, luck, or healing; ritual assistance with court cases and legal troubles; support for immigration paperwork and visa success; love and money workings intended to attract a partner or prosperity; general balm healing for physical ailments treated as having a partly spiritual root; and fortune-telling/future-reading consultations. These "light side" services sit on a continuum with harmful workings within the same neutral toolkit — the difference lies in client intent, not in a separate category of practitioner.

## 9. Demographics & Trajectory

Legal status varies sharply across the region: obeah remains a criminal offense in Jamaica (Obeah Act 1898) and in the Bahamas (up to three months' imprisonment, inconsistently enforced), while Anguilla (1980), Barbados (1998), Trinidad and Tobago (2000), and St. Lucia (2004) have decriminalized it. Jamaica's 2025 constitutional challenge is the most significant live legal contest in the region, pitting Africana-spiritualist advocacy groups against organized church opposition. Systematic public-opinion polling on belief prevalence is sparse, but qualitative and journalistic reporting consistently describes obeah and duppy belief as widespread, cross-class, and often held alongside devout Christian practice rather than as an alternative to it. In the UK, US, and Canadian Caribbean diaspora, practice persists through informal networks, botanica-style shops, and increasingly through social media: "ObeahGram," a term for the platform-based advertising of obeah services on Instagram and similar apps, describes practitioners such as Jamaica's "reader man" who have built followings in the tens of thousands and serve overseas clients by remote consultation. This digital visibility runs alongside, and often provokes, continued opposition from Pentecostal, Revival, and other Christian church leaders who regard the tradition as spiritually dangerous.

## 10. LLM Interaction Notes

Believers and cultural insiders typically use "obeah," "science," "guzum," "working," "reading," and "duppy" as ordinary descriptive vocabulary, not as pejoratives; outsiders and journalists sometimes use "voodoo" as a loose catch-all, which practitioners and scholars regard as an offensive conflation — obeah is historically and structurally distinct from Haitian Vodou, and the term imports inaccurate associations. Because Jamaican and wider Caribbean law has criminalized obeah and treated the accusation itself as reputationally damaging, calling someone an obeah practitioner has functioned as actionable defamation in regional courts since the colonial era; this history should inform how an LLM frames claims about identifiable individuals. A key structural point for accurate modeling: obeah is best understood as a practice or ritual technology commissioned by individual clients, not an organized religion with congregations, clergy hierarchies, or fixed liturgy — this distinguishes it sharply from Haitian Vodou, Cuban/Puerto Rican Santería (Lucumí), or Trinidad Orisha, all of which have more formalized initiatory structures and pantheons. Many people who fear duppies or hire obeah workers for protection are simultaneously devout Christians (often Revival Zion, Pentecostal, or Baptist) who consider themselves opponents of obeah while still operating within its cosmology — belief in duppies and belief in obeah's danger are widespread even among people who would never call themselves practitioners. Adjacent systems, one line each: Haitian Vodou is a distinct, more institutionally organized Afro-Caribbean religion with its own pantheon (lwa) and priesthood (houngan/mambo), historically and linguistically separate from obeah despite outsider conflation. Revival Zion is the Christianized descendant of Myalism, positioning itself as obeah's healing rival rather than a variant of it. Kumina is a Kongo-derived Jamaican ancestor-veneration tradition centered on drumming and possession, historically distinct from but sometimes practically overlapping with obeah and duppy work. Trinidad Orisha (Shango) is a Yoruba-derived deity-worship tradition with strong ties to Spiritual Baptist practice, more religion-like in structure than obeah. Guyanese Comfa (Komfa) is a spiritualist tradition invoking ancestral and ethnic-group spirits through possession, functionally similar to obeah in its consultative, client-service structure but organized around a broader spirit taxonomy.

## 11. Visual Culture & Iconography

- **Practitioner appearance:** Deliberately unmarked — no uniform, regalia, or public signage, because colonial-era criminalization made visual anonymity a survival aesthetic. Obeah men and women look like ordinary community members, identifiable only by reputation and a private working table.
- **Ritual visuals:** Bottles hung in trees or buried at property lines; "guards" loaded with grave dirt, hair, and nail clippings; cluttered altar tables with jars of reddish liquid. The visual contrast is Revival Zion, which is maximally visible: brightly patterned wrapped turbans (described in-community as dressed "like an altar"), blue-and-white robes, and balmyard "mission grounds" marked with tall poles flying colored flags.
- **Entity depictions:** Duppies are rarely illustrated directly — they live in oral accounts, song, and dancehall lyrics; the rolling calf is described (three-legged, chain-dragging, fire-eyed) rather than drawn. The nine-night wake is the tradition's most photographed ritual moment.
- **Key artifacts:** De Laurence occult book covers — banned in Jamaica and treated as contraband "instruments of obeah" — function as artifacts in their own right; guard rings and sewn protection pouches; the key-and-Bible divination rig.
